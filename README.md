# Overbuilt

Build any machine from parts, then take it anywhere and do anything with it. The game mixes the rocketry of *Spaceflight Simulator* and *Kerbal Space Program*, the free-form aircraft building of *SimplePlanes*, and the combat of *War Thunder*. It is free, and it runs in a browser on a PC, a phone or a tablet, with nothing to install.

You attach parts together on a blueprint, in 2D or in 3D: rockets, planes, helicopters, cars and armour (tanks, tank destroyers, armoured cars, SPAA, infantry vehicles, missile carriers, rocket artillery and SAM launchers), with engines, wings, tracks, turrets, weapons and paint wherever you want them. Then you fly or drive them in a full 3D world. Earth is the real one, shrunk to 1/20 scale, and the space centre sits on the Faroe Islands. From there you can:
- launch from the pad, reach orbit and explore a small solar system: land on the Moon, drive a rover, parachute onto Mars, dive past the Sun, and bring the crew home through a fiery reentry;
- fly planes that bank and turn over the islands and break the sound barrier, drive the causeway across the sea to Shetland, or hover a helicopter;
- drive a tank you built yourself onto Highland Ridge in North Scotland, where shells bounce off sloped plate, crews get knocked out and ammunition cooks off, in the spirit of War Thunder's ground battles;
- fight AI targets, fighters and computer-driven tanks, or other players online: in a free-for-all sandbox, in air battles where everyone builds on a budget and then fights, or in ground battles over capture zones with tickets and respawns.

It runs in any modern browser on desktop and mobile. There is no build step: open `index.html` and play. The 3D world uses three.js, which ships in the repo. On a device without WebGL the game falls back to a flat 2D side view (Armour then drives on the runway, and ground battles need the 3D view).

## Play

- **Online:** https://devwolf11.github.io/Overbuilt/ (deployed from `main` by `.github/workflows/pages.yml`). On a phone, use "Add to Home Screen" to install it and play offline.
- **Locally:** serve the folder (`npx serve .`). Offline play and installing need the HTTPS site above, because the service worker only registers over `https:`. Double-clicking `index.html` works too, but browsers don't let a page opened from disk read the Earth map, so Earth falls back to generated terrain.
- **Single file:** `node tools/build-single.mjs` writes `dist/overbuilt.html`, the whole game in one HTML file you can send to someone.

## Modes

- **Career:** start with $50,000 and basic parts. Every launch costs the price of its parts; recovering a craft on Earth refunds 90%. Complete 42 missions (first hop, orbit, flybys, orbits, landings and returns for every body, a Sun dive, interstellar escape, plus a first takeoff, a runway landing, the sound barrier, Mach 2, a seaplane round trip, a hover, a road trip by car or Armour, and three knock-outs on the Tank Range) to earn funds, and spend them in R&D to unlock five tiers of technology. A Practice Battle's vehicle is lent: it costs nothing and is never saved.
- **Sandbox:** every part unlocked and no money involved.
- **Multiplayer:** play with friends in your browsers, with no server and no accounts. See [Multiplayer](#multiplayer). The same screen has a **Practice battle (vs AI)**: a ground battle alone, with no room needed.

## Multiplayer

Press **Multiplayer** on the home screen, type a name, and either create a room or join one with its five-letter code. **Online** connects browsers directly to each other (WebRTC, through the free PeerJS service to find each other); **This device** joins other tabs of the same browser, which is handy for trying it alone or playing offline. The player who creates the room hosts it: their browser runs the match, and the room closes when they leave. The host's game lets friends in, so the host should keep it on screen while they join; on a phone it can't answer while the screen is off or another app is open (joining waits up to a minute for it to come back, says so, and the Join button turns into Cancel meanwhile). If joining fails, the message says at which step: no room with that code, the host's game not answering, or the two devices unable to connect (some routers, mobile networks, VPNs and school networks block direct links). **Test this network** on the multiplayer screen checks, in about 10 seconds, whether the matchmaking server, direct links and the relay server get through where you are. On networks that block direct links (UDP), such as many school networks, play goes through a relay (TURN) server instead, over port 443 like a website. The game uses a free relay account (Metered's Open Relay, with a monthly allowance; if it ever runs out, the relay stops until the next month and the test says so).

- **Sandbox** (up to 5 players): the normal world, every part, no rules. Build anything in the hangar and fly it into the shared sky; everyone sees everyone's craft, smoke and gunfire, and weapons work on each other. Crash, and you fly again or go back to the hangar. Everyone shares the host's clock, so the sun, the clouds and the time of day agree. Armour, and cars armed with the ground update's guns or launchers, start on Highland Ridge's two pads (the sides taken in turn) when the Earth map is in and the view is 3D; any other car, armed or not, starts on the runway as before.
- **Air battle** (1v1, 2v2, 3v3 or a six-player free-for-all; **Create air battle**): the host picks the format, the money ($45k, $75k or $120k), the build time (2, 3 or 5 minutes), whether there is a test flight, carriers, friendly fire, time of day and a time limit (10, 15 or 25 minutes). Then:
  1. **Build.** Everyone builds against the clock with the money they have; the stats show the cost against the budget. Air battles are for planes, helicopters and cars; ground battles (below) for Armour and cars, with aircraft only as air support. First pick a spawn on the map (War Thunder style): team Faroes starts on the space centre's runway or Carrier West, team Shetland at Sumburgh airfield on the southern tip of Shetland or Carrier East, and a free-for-all adds Wick airfield on the north coast of Scotland and Carrier South. A carrier gives 30% more money, but the craft has to fit the deck and the catapult: at most 13 m across, 16 m long and 12 tonnes, planes and helicopters only. Press Ready when you're done; the phase ends early once everyone is ready.
  2. **Test flight** (optional, one minute): fly your design alone, with nobody to shoot you, then get **two minutes to polish it**.
  3. **Battle.** Everyone spawns at once. One life each: the last team (or pilot) flying wins, and when the time runs out the side with the most craft still up wins, then the most kills. Shot down? Watch your teammates (`[` / `]`). A design that is over budget or breaks the carrier limits when the battle starts is swapped for a stock plane that fits.
- **Ground battle** (1v1, 2v2 or 3v3; **Create ground battle**, or Game: Ground in a battle's lobby): the War Thunder-style ground mode. The host picks the format, the money by era ($45k WWII, $75k Cold War, $120k Modern), the tickets (400, 600 or 900 a side), the respawns (Free, or a Lineup of 3), air support (on or off), the aim (Arcade or Realistic), then the build time, a test drive (a minute on your pad, then two to polish), friendly fire, time of day and the time limit. The arena is the host's map's: **Highland Ridge**, rolling ground in North Scotland measured from the real map, or the space centre's runway when the Earth map didn't load (the lobby warns a player whose map differs from the host's). Ground battles need the 3D view.
  1. **Build** Armour or a car against the clock; with a Lineup of 3, pick two more vehicles for slots 2 and 3 (saved or stock designs). A design over the money when the battle starts is swapped for the era's stock tank, never a plane.
  2. **Fight.** Each team starts on its own levelled pad, the two 2.4 km apart and out of each other's sight, facing three zones: the Hilltop (A), which overlooks the others, the Slope (C) in the middle and the East ground (B). A tank or car under 30 m/s takes a neutral zone in 20 s alone, 1.5 times as fast with two, 1.8 with three or more, and half as fast again with living troops aboard; an enemy zone goes neutral at the halfway mark and is yours after 40 s. Both sides inside freeze it. The side holding fewer zones bleeds the difference in tickets every second, and every vehicle lost costs its team 30 tickets plus one for every $2,500 it cost (half as much again for an aircraft). Out of tickets, or with nobody left who can respawn, a team loses; on time, more tickets wins. A 1v1 is fought over zone C alone, 70 m across. Twelve earth berms give hull-down cover, and leaving the battle area brings a warning every 5 s.
  3. **Knocked out?** Your hulk stays where it stopped, on every screen, as a wreck that blocks shells and gives cover (at most eight wrecks, each for 90 s; an ammunition detonation throws its turret). After 5 s the **Respawn** panel offers your vehicle, another of your designs or a stock one within the money, or, with air support on and a sortie point earned by knock-outs and captures, an aircraft (one a flight, a minute apart), which joins 1,000 m up at 140 m/s behind your pad. A Lineup of 3 offers the slots you haven't used. A fresh spawn can't be hurt for 4 s.
  - **Spotting:** an enemy's red tag shows only while you or a teammate can see it: within 250 m, in your line of sight within 1.5 km (a quarter further with a living commander; 3 km between the ground and the air) with hills, wrecks and smoke in the way (a Thermal Sight sees through smoke), for 3 s after a teammate sees it (15 s after `J`, or a long press on its tag), and for 3 s after it fires its main gun within 2.5 km. A tap on the tactical map pings a spot for your team.
  - **Score:** the scoreboard reads Kills, Caps, Lost and Score, the bar shows both teams' tickets round the zone chips and a capture bar while you stand in a zone, and the results say how it ended ("Faroes win on tickets (212 – 0) · Highland Ridge").
- **Practice battle (vs AI)** on the multiplayer screen: the ground battle alone on your device, against the computer. Pick the era (it says which vehicle you'll drive: your blueprint's design if it fits the money, else the era's stock tank); you and two AI allies on the north pad fight three AI vehicles from the south pad for the zones, 600 tickets a side, with the same rules, wrecks and a Respawn panel. Revert to launch puts you back on your pad for the vehicle's tickets; there is no Recover.
- **Carriers:** catapult launches. Start the engine and go to full throttle, then hit **LAUNCH** (`L`): the catapult throws the plane off the bow at about 280 km/h. Land on a deck with the throttle back and the arrestor wires stop you. Helicopters start on the aft deck.
- **Seeing the others:** names float over everyone's craft in team colours (blue friends, red enemies, gold in the sandbox). Enemies only show their tag within about 6 km, less if they are stealthy (in a ground battle, only while spotted: above). With a warning receiver (RWR) on board, a radar lock on you flashes RADAR LOCK, and the Advanced RWR's scope shows where it comes from.
- **Chat and score:** `T` or `Enter` chats in flight, the lobby has a chat box, a kill feed runs down the side, and the scoreboard (hold `` ` ``, or the Score button) shows kills, deaths and damage.

## What's in it

- **The real Earth:** real mountains, sea floors, coastlines and colours on the 3D globe, the ground you fly over and the map, from about 5 km per height sample. The real rivers carve their valleys (the Nile through Cairo, the Amazon past Manaus, the Seine through Paris) and the big lakes sit at their real levels (Lake Victoria, the Great Lakes, Lake Geneva); seaplanes can land on them and roads cross them on bridges. Real motorways and roads cross the land, levelled into the hills. About 6,500 real towns and cities stand where they are, as full-size streets of houses and blocks of flats with a downtown of towers in the big ones, farmland makes a patchwork of fields around them, forests grow where the satellite photo is green and snow lies where it is white. At night the cities light up, on the ground and seen from orbit. The HUD says where you are ("Over France · Paris"). The space centre stands on the west coast of the Faroe Islands (62°N, 7°W), green, treeless and ringed with basalt sea cliffs, with a lighthouse, wind turbines on the hills, a tank farm and a seaplane pier. Its runway 12/30 runs ESE across the islands, and the causeway carries on 18 km over the sea to a port town on Shetland. Sumburgh airfield sits on Shetland's southern tip and Wick airfield on the north coast of Scotland, each with its own runway, lights, hangars and tower, and three aircraft carriers ride at anchor in the sea between them, with a flight deck you can land on (the arrestor wires catch you) and catapults to launch from. The game keeps its pad on its own equator, so a launch to the east goes into an orbit inclined 62° over the real Earth, as it would from the real Faroes. Villages dot the land and fishing boats and freighters the sea.
- **A 3D world:** craft move and turn in all three dimensions: orbits can be inclined, planes bank and turn anywhere over the island, cars drive off across the countryside. You see textured planets from space, terrain with oceans, beaches, trees and clouds near the ground, the space centre with its runway, helipad and causeway, shadows, engine plumes and smoke. The camera follows behind planes, cars and helicopters and beside rockets.
- **Vehicles:** rockets launch from the pad, planes take off from the runway, cars drive from it (a causeway road leads 18 km across the sea to Shetland), helicopters lift off from the helipad, and Armour starts on Highland Ridge's north pad (below). Wings make lift from the airflow and stall at high angles; ailerons roll, elevators pitch and the tail fin's rudder yaws and keeps a plane pointing into the wind, so banked turns are coordinated. Propellers and jets need air, rotors give lift and the stick tilts a helicopter (it levels itself when you let go), car wheels drive and the front ones steer, landing gear rolls freely and everything brakes. Planes hold their pitch and bank when you let go, or the autopilot can level them out or hold a heading. In every autopilot mode the rudder keeps turns coordinated, so the plane doesn't skid or fishtail. The ailerons roll more gently when the plane is slow or already sliding sideways, and the nose comes down before a climb runs out of speed. Off is the bare stick, with none of that help.
- **Weapons and combat:** machine guns, a 30 mm autocannon, a seven-barrel rotary cannon and gun pods; rocket pods; heat-seeking missiles, radar missiles (they reach far only with a radar in the nose), attack missiles, anti-radar missiles that home on radars, SAM sites, AA guns and frigates, and a cruise missile that flies itself to a target 30 km away; 250 kg bombs, a 1-tonne bomb, an internal bomb bay, cluster bombs that burst into 24 bomblets, guided glide bombs and torpedoes that swim to boats and ships; flares, a chaff dispenser whose bundles burst into glittering foil that fools radar missiles, an ECM jammer pod (incoming SAMs lose you, AA gunners aim badly, radar locks take longer), two radar warning receivers (below) and a manned gun turret, big enough for its gunner, that turns and fires by itself at fighters and drones, but only in the air: touching the ground or the water it parks its guns and holds its fire, so a field of turrets round a parked plane, or on a car, can't shoot down whatever comes near. Guns fire tracers (hold `V`), missiles lock on to what's in front of the nose (a growl, then a steady tone and LOCK), bombs fall where the bombsight on the HUD shows, and the gun pipper and a lead marker help you aim. Stores leave their pylons as they go, and the craft gets lighter.
- **Air-to-air missiles can be beaten** (in the spirit of War Thunder):
  - **Energy:** the motor burns for a few seconds, then the missile coasts and loses speed to drag (more in the thick air down low) and to every turn. Its fins only pull their full g while it is fast, so a long or low shot can fall short.
  - **Seekers:** a missile sees only so far off its nose and can only swing its seeker so fast. It arms after 0.6 s.
  - **Heat seekers** lock at a range set by the target's heat: a jet at full power from behind at 5 km, one at idle or coming head-on at 1 to 2 km, a propeller plane closer still. Flares (`K`) fool them, far more often when the target throttles back than in afterburner.
  - **Radar missiles** need your nose radar on the target the whole way. If you turn away, they go dumb. A target that dives low and flies across your beam ("notching") or drops chaff can shake them, and a jammer pod slows a radar lock. Chaff from a **Chaff Dispenser** (24 bundles) is far stronger than the cloud that comes free with flares: it works best once the missile is within a few kilometres and you fly across its path. `K` (or the CM button) drops flares and a chaff bundle together. The HUD shows ✦ flares and ≋ chaff left, and with an Advanced RWR the warning reads RADAR MISSILE when a radar-guided one is after you, so you know to drop chaff or notch. The Falcon, Kestrel, Striker and Spectre carry a dispenser, and enemy fighters drop chaff against your radar missiles too.
  - **Warnings come from a part:** only a radar warning receiver (RWR, in the Weapons tab) tells you a missile is homing on you, solo and in multiplayer, heat seekers too. The **Basic RWR** ($1,000) flashes MISSILE with a warning tone (and RADAR LOCK when another player's radar holds you), but not what kind of missile or where from. The **Advanced RWR** ($3,000) adds a threat display: a small scope with every missile round your craft (IR a heat seeker, R a radar missile) and every radar lock on its rim, and over the nose the nearest missile's kind, distance and seconds to impact, and what to do ("FLARES NOW · break turn", "put it on your wing and dive (notch)", "CHAFF NOW"). Flying solo, with one on board, your countermeasures go out by themselves: the right decoy with the Advanced RWR, flares and chaff together with the basic one, which can't tell them apart. Without one nothing warns you, and nothing goes out by itself: look around, because every hostile missile in sight within 4 km has a red MSL marker over it, like the markers over other planes. The stock military planes carry one (the basic one up to the 3rd generation, the advanced one after); the warbirds don't.
- **Targets around the islands:** tanks, trucks, radars, bunkers, AA guns and SAM sites on the Faroes and Shetland, patrol boats and a frigate at sea and target drones in the air, each bracketed on the HUD with its range. Settings › Targets: Practice (they just take it), Hostile (AA guns fire back, the frigate fires its guns and radar-guided SAMs that chaff, notching or an island between you can beat, SAM sites launch heat seekers that flares can fool, and enemy fighters come hunting, flying the same physics as you) or Off. Destroyed targets come back elsewhere a minute later. Near Highland Ridge, a ground vehicle gets the Tank Range instead (below), from the same setting.
- **Damage:** every part has health. Bullets, shells, rockets and explosions wear it down; a damaged engine loses thrust and smokes, then burns, a damaged wing loses lift, holed tanks leak, and a part at zero breaks off or blows up (fuel and ammunition go up with it). A hard landing dents parts instead of wrecking them; only a real crash breaks them outright. The HUD shows how much of the craft is damaged. An armoured fuselage turns away 60% of the damage.
- **Stealth:** stealth noses, fuselages, the internal bomb bay and the internal missile bays hide a plane from radar (a bay's doors spoil it only for the second or two they are open to fire): the more of its hull is stealthy, the closer enemy SAMs, AA guns and fighters have to be to find it (the weapon box shows it as ◈). Bombs, missiles, pods and tanks hung outside spoil it.
- **Structure:** every wing has a load limit and a speed limit. Sturdy fighter wings take 8 g, a glider's give way at a fraction of that, and an airliner sits in between. The HUD shows the g-load against what the wings can take, and a warning (with a beep) comes up before they give. A moment over the limit is survivable: stress builds up while you hold it and fades once you ease off, so a hard pull in a sturdy plane is fine and only a held over-g (or a far-over one) bends the wings and tears them off.
  - **Speed:** push a wing past its speed and it flutters: the view shakes, the wind roars, and within a few seconds (at once, far past it) it tears off. An overspeed warning comes up from 90% of the limit, and "slow down" once it starts to flutter. Biplane wings go at about 510–590 km/h, gliders at 500–600, straight wings (plain, elliptical, small) at 790–900 km/h and the transport wing at 700–800, at any height planes fly at. Airliner wings take 950 km/h low down and just past Mach 1 up high. Swept, forward-swept and swing wings are good for Mach 1.7–2.5 up high, the delta for Mach 2–3, tails and strakes for more still.
  - The supersonic wings' limits are in sea-level km/h: in thinner air high up they can go faster, as on a real plane. In the very thin air above about 20 km only that limit counts, so a wing can't shake apart up there; coming down too fast, the warning shows while there is still time to slow down. The builder shows each wing's limit and each design's g-limit and breaking speed. The Barnstormer is built from two plain wings, so it flies to the plain wing's limit; the Camel has the braced biplane wings.
- **Look-alike jets:** ten fighters from four generations, shaped after real ones under names of their own: the MyG-21 and FS-4 (3rd generation), the FS-16, MyG-29, FS-15 and Sugoi-27 (4th), the Sugoi-35 (4.5) and the FS-22, FS-35 and Sugoi-57 (5th). Each is built at the real aircraft's size and lightly loaded weight from parts shaped like the real ones (the same shape in the 2D blueprint and in 3D), wears its own camouflage and reaches about the real one's top speed: Mach 2 and more up high, and a little past Mach 1 on the deck, where full afterburner sets off the overspeed warning but doesn't tear the wings. Their parts sit in the tray with the others, so they mix with anything.
  - **5th generation:** the FS-22, FS-35 and Sugoi-57 carry their missiles inside, and the bay doors snap open only to fire, so they stay stealthy until they shoot. The FS-22 cruises past Mach 1.5 without the afterburner, and its flat nozzles swing up and down with the stick, pitching the nose round even at low speed; the Sugoi-35 and Sugoi-57 have nozzles that swivel with it.
  - **Battles:** every one of them fits a $120k battle exactly as it comes (the MyG-21 fits a $45k one and the FS-16 a $75k one), priced by generation, from $45,000 to $120,000 (each with its warning receiver), and rivals are matched (the FS-15 and Sugoi-27, the FS-16 and MyG-29, the FS-22 and Sugoi-57).
- **Armour (the ground update):** a fifth kind of craft, for tanks, tank destroyers, armoured cars, SPAA, infantry fighting vehicles, anti-tank missile carriers, rocket artillery and SAM launchers, all built from parts, in the spirit of War Thunder's ground battles but your own design. 71 parts in seven tabs (Hulls, Turrets, Drive, Guns, Launchers, Crew & ammo, Kit), grouped in the tray by era (WWII, Cold War, Modern, AA). Cars can carry the new guns too; old cars drive exactly as before.
  - **Building:** six taps make a working tank, because every part goes where it belongs on a tap: a hull on the blueprint, a track beside it with its twin on the other side, the engine inside at the back, a turret ring on the roof, the turret on the ring and the gun in the turret's front, breech inside, pointing forward. Launchers go on a bare ring or beside the gun, seats and racks into the first free room inside the armour, radars to the back of the roof, smoke launchers in pairs on the turret's sides, bricks and plates flush on the face nearest the middle of the view, skirts and slat cages over the tracks.
  - **Customising:** every hull, turret and plate has a thickness you dial in 5 mm steps (the Armour row: − and + or the slider), and the mass, price and speed follow it; under it each face reads what it comes to against a level shot ("Glacis 55 mm at 63° → 121 mm"). Hulls and turrets come with their crew, ammunition, fuel and troops built in; placed seats and racks take over, so you decide where the crew sit and where the shells are (a wet rack cooks off less, an autoloader carousel needs no loader). X-ray (`X`, on by default) shows them through the plates in 2D and 3D, the armour view colours every plate by its thickness, a picked ring or gun shows its traverse and elevation fans (red where something stops it), and Test turret swings the turret once through its arc in 3D.
  - **The stats speak tank:** mass, power to weight, top speed and the front's thickness, then a line such as "85 mm APHE 124 mm at 1 km · reload 7.0 s · turret 24°/s · gun −5°/+22° · crew 5 · pivots 40°/s · climbs 30° · tips at 0.90 g · 42 rounds", and warnings: no tracks or tracks on one side, no engine or too little, overloaded running gear, no driver, gunner or loader, a rack outside the armour, a turret that stops (and what stops it), a gun that dips into the back deck, top-heavy, a radar SAM with no tracking radar, tracks that don't reach the ground. Only a missing hull stops a launch.
  - **Stock vehicles:** fifteen under altered names, each blurb teaching one thing: the TK-34, MS-4 Shermin, Tygr I, Pumo 8×8, StuQ III and MS-18 Hellion (WWII), the Leapard 1, TK-72, BMK-2, BRDK-2 Gadfly and BK-21 Grod (Cold War), the MA-1 Abrahm (modern), and the ZSY-23 Shilko, Gepardo and OK-8 Hive (air defence), from $18,800 to $79,300; the hangar lists them first while you build Armour, each with its class. Paint them in the four armour paints: Steppe green, and Ambush, Woodland and Winter whitewash three-tone camouflage.
- **Driving:** one track part a side, skid-steered by the two belts' speeds: A or D alone turns on the spot (and the brakes hold it there on a slope), A or D while moving turns as it rolls, and reversing turns like a car's tail. Engines are rated in kW (a 260 kW petrol engine to a 1,100 kW turbine), so acceleration, top speed and hill climbing follow power to weight, and traction is limited by grip. The suspension is soft and sprung: the hull squats under power, dips when braking and rocks back when the gun fires. Armoured wheels steer by the front axle like a car's and run twice as fast on the flat (the Pumo 8×8 does 108 km/h). Hulls wade at 2 m/s and the troop and wheeled hulls swim at 3.5 m/s; deeper than 1.4 m over the engine floods it ("Engine flooded!"). A broken track drags and the hull swings round it. Rolled onto its side or roof, a helping hand rights it within seconds ("Righting…"). Vehicles bump into each other and into wrecks, and ramming hard dents what met. Tracks throw dust, and the engine's note follows the power it gives.
- **Aiming:** the camera sits behind and over the turret and looks where you aim, so the crosshair is the aim; the turret follows at its ring's pace (slower with a heavy turret, slower still with the ring damaged, cranked by hand with it broken, not at all when something jams it) and the gun elevates within its limits, while a small reticle shows where the gun really points as it catches up and fills as it reloads. Casemate guns have a small arc of their own (turn the hull for the rest), and stabilised guns hold their aim on the move where others bounce off it. The gunner's sight zooms 3×, 6× or 8× with mil ticks, and opens (and closes) on the point the crosshair was on, so a tank picked out from behind the turret is still under it in the sight. **Arcade aim** (the default) lifts the gun for the range under the crosshair; **Realistic aim** lifts it for the sight's range (Zero), which a laser rangefinder or, after 3 s on one target, the commander's reading sets, with stadia for the loaded shell. A Blocked reticle says when the barrel would hit a crest the camera sees past. Aim assist (on by default on touch) eases the crosshair onto an aircraft's lead point.
- **Armour and damage:** every plate has its thickness in mm, and its slope comes from the part's outline, which is both its 3D model and what shells hit, so a sloped glacis really is thicker against a level shot. 37 kinds of round (AP, APHE, APCR, APDS, APFSDS, HEAT, HESH, HE, HEI, proximity HE and machine-gun ball) ricochet off steep plates by their type, overmatch thin ones, and lose penetration with speed (shaped charges don't). Skirts set shaped charges off early, slat cages crush half of them, reactive bricks blow apart the first shaped charge or dart that hits them (tandem warheads get through), and an APHE shell bursts inside. Behind the armour sit the crew and the modules: a dead driver or gunner's seat is taken over after 4 s (by the loader, the radio operator or the commander), and with fewer than two crew left the vehicle is knocked out and its hulk stays. Ammunition cooks off and throws the turret (a blow-out bustle vents the blast and the crew lives), engines and fuel catch fire, and tracks, engines, breeches, rings, radars, sights and launchers break and are mended one at a time once you stop, unhurt for 6 s ("Repairing: left track 8 s"). High explosive overmatches the thinnest plate it faces, and open tops are open. Old weapons work on armour by their size (bullets, cannon, rockets, missiles, bombs), and the new guns still shred planes and a technical's cab.
- **SPAA, SAMs, missiles, rocket artillery and smoke:** a search radar shows every aircraft within 10 km on a scope; a tracking radar locks one (LOCK, or `L`), lights its warning receiver and leads the guns onto it. 35 mm shells burst as they pass an aircraft, and a quad 23 mm throws a wall of them. Heat-seeking SAMs lock as you aim (flares fool them), command-guided SAMs ride the tracking radar's beam while you hold the lock (chaff and a dive to the treetops can break it), and long-range SAMs home on the radar's lock; all boost off their rails. Wire-guided anti-tank missiles fly where the crosshair points until they hit; a twin launcher's tandem warheads get through reactive bricks; a top-attack missile locks on and dives onto the roof (a turbine tank's hot exhaust is seen further off); none of them sets off a warning receiver. Rocket artillery ripples 16 rockets from rails, or six heavy ones to about 8 km from a pod, laid at the crosshair or a spot tapped on the tactical map, and says so when a crest is in the way ("Blocked by the ground 180 m out"). Smoke launchers (`K`, two salvos) throw a screen ahead of the turret for 25 s that hides you from eyes, heat seekers and tracking radars, and a wire-guided missile steered through it flies on blind; a Thermal Sight sees through it.
- **Highland Ridge and the Tank Range:** with the Earth map in and the 3D view on, Armour launches onto the north pad of Highland Ridge in North Scotland (without them, onto the runway, with a note why). Its ground is made for the fight: levelled pads, twelve earth berms 2.4 m high for hull-down cover, no trees or houses, and a ground mesh of its own built from the very heights the physics stands on, so the crest you see is the crest your shell hits. Settings › Targets runs the **Tank Range** there: Practice puts out five targets that never shoot (a TK-34 side-on, an MS-4 hull-down behind a berm, a ZSY-23 on the hilltop, and a Pumo and a Leapard 1 on patrol), each back somewhere else 45 s after you knock it out, plus two target drones for an AA vehicle; Hostile sends waves of three to five fighting stock vehicles priced near yours from the south pad, and, for anything with AA aboard, an attack plane a minute in and every three minutes after; Off, nothing. The end screen counts your knock-outs and your best shot.
  - **Computer tanks** are real stock vehicles with the same armour, crews and guns as yours: they drive round obstacles, stop behind berms to shoot inside 1.2 km after a moment's reaction, flank after three harmless hits, pop smoke and back off when hurt, go home when they lose their gun or most of their crew (and come back once it's mended), load AP for armour and HE for open tops, check their shot clears the ground, and never see through hills or smoke.
- **The ground HUD:** the crosshair with the range under it, the gun's reticle, a reload ring round FIRE, the shell chips (tap one to load it next, hold it to load it now), what each shot did ("Ricochet!", "Did not penetrate", "Hit · tracks", "Penetration · 2 crew"), red arcs toward whoever hit you, "Knocked out!" and "Ammo detonation!", a damage diagram from above (the turret as it points, the crew where they sit, broken modules lit, the repair counting down), the lead diamond, lock box and radar scope of an SPAA, GUIDING for a missile, a rocket strike's ring and time, zone badges and capture rings on the ground, tags for the enemies you see, a north-up minimap and a full-screen tactical map with hillshade (`M` or MAP; tap it to ping, mark a spot or set a rocket strike; its Globe button opens the old map). The autopilot buttons, the flight rows (altitude, Mach, g, vertical speed) and the prograde markers give way to a crew count and a speed that says which way you're going (▲ forward, ▼ in reverse); the end screen says "Knocked out" or "Off the range", with the distance driven. It is laid out to stay clear of itself on a phone in portrait or on its side, on a desktop and in a room, and writes nothing to the page while nothing changes. A tank gun's report thumps and cracks, duller and later the further off it is.
- **The sound barrier:** past Mach 1 in thick air a vapour cone wraps the craft, a shock ring bursts outwards and a sonic boom cracks; the HUD shows the Mach number.
- **328 parts** in twenty-one categories (327 in the tray: the first, small gun turret has retired, and old designs keep it): crew pods and probe cores; tanks in 1, 2 and 3 m widths; nine rocket engines including solid boosters that can't be shut down, an aerospike that keeps its efficiency at every altitude, a nuclear engine and an ion drive; separators; nose cones, fins and heat shields; parachutes, landing legs, rover wheels and RCS; girders and adapters; ground parts (driver cab, chassis, car wheels); 182 aircraft parts and 31 weapons (96 of these 213 come from the ten look-alike jets); and 71 armour parts in the seven Armour tabs (the last seven bullets below):
  - **Cockpits:** bush, standard, fighter-nose and airliner cockpits, a two-seat tandem trainer canopy, a faceted stealth nose with a gold canopy, a glazed bomber nose, a warbird cockpit section, an all-glass helicopter bubble and a pilotless drone core; and the look-alike jets' noses: a shock-cone intake nose, a twin-seat nose with a black radome, bubble-canopy noses, drooping heavy-fighter noses and chined stealth noses.
  - **Fuselage:** a radome, plain, long, slim and wide fuselages, a cargo hull with a loading ramp, a fuel-tank fuselage, an armoured tub, a faceted stealth body, a fuselage with cheek intakes, a slim tail boom for helicopters and twin-boom planes, two adapters, four tail cones and a spinning radar rotodome that triples how far the HUD sees targets; and the look-alike jets' bodies, shaped like the real ones: blended and flat lifting fuselages, chin, box, caret, bump and splitter-plate intakes, intake nacelles, tail booms, stingers and beavertails, and an IRST ball that lets heat seekers lock on 30% further away.
  - **Wings:** short, standard and long wings (with flaps and fuel), swept, delta and glider wings, a swing wing that folds back by itself as you speed up, a forward-swept wing, an elliptical wing, braced biplane wings, a long straight transport wing, an airliner wing with winglets, strakes for flying at wild angles, two tailplanes, two elevators, a canard, a V-tail that pitches and yaws at once, four tail fins including a twin-tail pair, and air brakes; and each look-alike jet's own wing, tailplanes and fins: a cropped delta, a dog-tooth wing whose outer panels tilt up, curved root extensions running into a swept wing, LEVCONs, canted twin tails and all-moving fins.
  - **Aero engines:** small, standard and four-blade propellers, a V12 behind a pointed spinner, contra-rotating props (no torque roll), a ducted fan, an electric motor with its own battery, a turboprop, a pusher prop, twin wing-mounted props, a pulsejet, small and standard turbojets, an afterburning jet, a fighter turbofan, a thrust-vectoring jet, jet pods, standard and geared turbofans and fan pods of each, a ramjet that only lights at speed, a scramjet for Mach 3 to 7, a rocket motor for X-planes, JATO bottles and helicopter rotors; and the look-alike jets' engines: turbojets and turbofans in ones and pairs, nozzles that swivel with the stick, flat vectoring nozzles that pitch the plane round when the wings can't, and a sawtooth stealth nozzle that hides some heat.
  - **Gear:** small, standard and heavy gear, small, standard and heavy retractable gear, a four-wheel airliner bogie, fat tundra tyres, a tail wheel, helicopter skids, floats, drop tanks, a brake chute and an air-show smoke generator; and gear legs and stealth gear that folds away behind doors.
  - **Weapons:** machine gun, autocannon, rotary cannon, gun pods, a manned gun turret, rocket pods, heat-seeking, radar, attack, anti-radar and cruise missiles, bombs, a heavy bomb, an internal bomb bay, cluster bombs, glide bombs, torpedoes, flares, a chaff dispenser, an ECM jammer and basic and advanced radar warning receivers; and internal missile bays whose doors open only to fire, internal 20 mm cannons, a chin gun and a pair of early heat seekers.

Each is the vehicle's control part, with its crew, shells and fuel inside.
  - **Turrets (11):** four turret rings (small, standard, heavy, and a powered one that keeps the turret stabilised on target) and seven turrets: a one-man small turret, a rounded cast one, a box of upright plates, a modern composite wedge with its rounds in a bustle behind blow-out panels, a low autoloader turret, an open-topped one and an AA turret.

  - **Guns (16):** 37, 50, 75 (stabilised), 76, 85 and 88 mm guns, a 105 mm rifled gun, 120 and 125 mm smoothbores, a 152 mm howitzer, 20 and 30 mm autocannons, a 35 mm AA gun with proximity shells, a quad 23 mm AA mount, a coaxial MG and a heavy MG on its own swivel.   - **Launchers (8):** a wire-guided missile rail, a twin launcher with tandem warheads, a top-attack launcher, an IR SAM rack, a radar-commanded SAM launcher, a long-range SAM launcher, rocket rails and a rocket artillery pod.
  - **Crew & ammo (10):** driver's, gunner's, commander's and loader's seats, ammo racks (standard, large and water-jacketed), an autoloader carousel, a troop bay and a fuel cell that stops fragments.

- **Flight model:** wings and tail fins mount straight through the fuselage, and pods, floats and drop tanks hang off them in mirrored pairs (engine pods fixed to the fuselage instead get pylons). Each wing gets its own lift slope from its aspect ratio and sweep, induced drag (less for an elliptical wing or winglets, more for a biplane, which also drags its struts and wires), a stall angle, dihedral that rolls you level, ground effect and transonic wave drag; a swing wing's sweep, lift and wave drag change with the Mach number; flaps add lift and drag, control surfaces stiffen at high speed, and pulling too many g tears wings and tails off. Jets gain thrust with ram air and afterburners kick in above 90% throttle, turbofans lose thrust as they speed up, single props twist the plane (contra-rotating ones don't), props break if they strike the ground, retracted gear stops dragging and seaplanes take off from the bay.
- **Builder:** build on the 2D blueprint or in 3D (mouse or touch). The tray shows the parts for the kind of craft you're building (a plane gets cockpits, fuselage, wings, aero engines, gear and weapons; a rocket command pods, tanks, engines, couplings, aero, utility and structure; Armour hulls, turrets, drive, guns, launchers, crew & ammo and kit); More shows the other tabs too, since any part still goes on any craft, and each look-alike jet's parts sit together, labelled with the jet. Parts attach to whatever they're dropped on, exactly where they're dropped: under a wing, on a fuselage's flank or back, or on the end of another part. Parts go as close as their 3D shapes let them, not their boxes: a round hull is round, so a pod, an engine or a gun put beside a fuselage or a nose cone, in 2D or 3D, settles onto its curve rather than out at the corner of a box, and two round tanks can sit corner to corner. Whatever hangs on a part moves, turns and goes with it (move a wing and its engines and missiles come along; delete it and they go too). Every part has a depth across the craft, so engines, pods, missiles and bombs can go anywhere along a wing, as many as fit: in 3D just drop them there; on the 2D side view, a part dropped onto a wing finds the first free spot along it, Duplicate puts another one further out, and `[` `]` or the Depth slider (Spread, for a pair) move the selected part in and out. Parts behind one another show a ×count in the side view, and a tap on the selected one picks the one behind. A Grid button (`G`, 2D only) switches the old quarter-metre grid back on. Paint: every part can wear one of 30 paints, or its own colours: thirteen plain ones (gloss white, greys, stealth grey, night black, navy, blue, olive drab, desert tan, red, orange, yellow, bare metal), the look-alike jets' thirteen, eleven of them camouflage (two-tone greys, splinter, digital, frontline and jungle patterns), and four for armour (Steppe green, and Ambush, Woodland and Winter whitewash three-tone camouflage, its patches sized for a tank), all laid on in metres, so the patches run on from one part to the next; Match (`M`) paints a part like the one it hangs on, All paints the whole craft like the selected part, and a new part takes the paint of what it's put on, so a wing on a stealth fuselage comes in stealth grey. Paint is only looks: radar stealth still comes from the stealth parts. Quarter-turn part rotation, symmetry (mirrored parts turn the other way; for planes, whatever goes out on one wing gets a twin on the other), automatic staging with per-stage Δv and TWR, centre-of-mass and thrust markers, cost and tech-tier locks in career, undo/redo. Shift-click (or Select mode, for touch) picks several parts, or drag a box round them; drag any of them to move them together, and copy, cut and paste them (`Ctrl+C` / `Ctrl+X` / `Ctrl+V`, also into another design), turn them, nudge them or delete them as a group. `Ctrl+A` selects everything. The Save button by the design's name keeps it in the hangar (`Ctrl+S`; tap the name to save a copy under a new name), with a dot when there are unsaved changes; the hangar lists your designs with thumbnails, warns before replacing one with the same name, and asks before loading over unsaved work. Any design can be copied as a short text code and pasted into any copy of the game.
- **Physics:** 3D rigid bodies with a full inertia tensor and quaternion attitude, gimbal and reaction wheels, pressure-dependent Isp, drag and fin stability, wings simulated in halves so rolling damps itself, reentry heating that burns away the leading part unless a heat shield takes it, parachutes limited by dynamic pressure (drogue first, main canopy near the ground), oceans you can splash down in, rover driving with steering, self-levelling and a hand back onto the wheels if it tips over, and per-corner ground contact on the 3D terrain where hard landings break parts. A rocket's legs and fins stand in fours around the stack.
- **Solar system:** the Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth and the Moon, Mars and Phobos, Jupiter and Europa, each with its own gravity, terrain, colours, and atmosphere where it has one. Patched conics with spheres of influence; time warp up to 1,000,000× runs craft on exact Kepler orbits and stops automatically at atmospheres and sphere changes.
- **Navigation:** the map opens on a 3D globe of the planet you're at, north up, with a grid of real latitude and longitude, countries, seas and cities, the space centre, the track you've flown and where you're heading (a great circle with a tick for every minute at your speed) or the orbit you're on, drawn in 3D over the ground. Drag to turn it, pinch or scroll to zoom from the whole planet down to a few kilometres, where it paints the ground as the game shapes it; a strip shows your latitude and longitude and the distance and bearing home. The HUD shows a compass heading for planes, cars and helicopters. `N` (or the System button) switches to the solar-system map, which predicts up to four patches ahead, showing apoapsis and periapsis, encounters (with the periapsis you'll get), escapes and impacts. Tap a planet to target it and see the closest approach and when the next transfer window opens. Autopilot modes hold prograde, retrograde, radial or target, and prograde/retrograde markers sit around the craft. A Δv map covers every destination.
- **Graphics:** day and night as Earth circles the Sun, sunsets and sun glare, stars and a faint Milky Way, oceans with surf and beaches, lit cumulus clouds you can fly through, trees, rocks, villages with lit windows at night, a launch site with a tower and buildings, shaded planets with atmospheres, Mars' ice caps and Jupiter's bands, engine plumes per propellant with shock diamonds, smoke, dust kicked off the ground, reentry plasma, heat glow on parts, explosions with flashes and shockwaves.
- **Saving:** designs and flights save automatically (also when a phone backgrounds the page). Resume a flight from the home screen. A tank's saved flight keeps its armour, its turret's angle, what is broken, who took a dead crewman's seat and the rounds left in each rack. A Practice Battle is never saved: its vehicle is lent.
- **Settings:** volume, graphics quality (shadows, scenery, effects), frame rate, flight hints, markers, ground aim assist (on by default on touch), Arcade or Realistic ground aim flying solo (a ground battle's lobby sets it for a room), the aim's speed (the mouse's, or a finger's drag on a phone), fullscreen, career reset. The 3D view lowers its resolution on its own when frames run long and raises it again when there is time to spare.
- **Battery:** the game only does work that changes what you see. Still screens (the hangar, the 2D blueprint, menus) stop redrawing until something changes, the home screen draws at 30 fps, and a paused flight or a dialog over one at 10 fps. In flight it skips the water, sky and planet hidden behind the ground, redraws no shadows at night, paints Earth's map first and the other planets after it, a little each frame, and runs physics at exactly 120 steps a second. Sound sleeps outside flight and while paused, and the screen is no longer kept awake while paused. **Frame rate** (Settings) caps the game at 60 fps by default on phones and tablets, so a 120 Hz screen does half the work; set it to Unlimited for the smoothest picture. In flight the HUD panels are a touch more opaque instead of blurring the moving view behind them. Highland Ridge's own ground mesh is built a few milliseconds a frame, and only in ground play (a ground battle, the Practice Battle, the Tank Range, or Armour on show), so a plane flying past from Wick pays nothing for it; the ground HUD writes nothing to the page while nothing changes, and the minimap redraws at most four times a second, only when it changed.

## Scale

Like Spaceflight Simulator, the solar system is shrunk. Earth has a 315 km radius, real surface gravity (9.8 m/s²) and a 30 km atmosphere; low orbit takes about 2.7 km/s of Δv. The Moon orbits 6,200 km out. Earth circles the Sun at 200,000 km in about 6.5 days, so a trip to Mars takes about four and a half days of game time, a few seconds at full warp.

Scripts are plain (non-module) JavaScript so the game also works when opened straight from disk.

The Earth map is baked from open data: elevation and sea-floor depth from the [AWS Terrain Tiles](https://registry.opendata.aws/terrain-tiles/) (Mapzen terrarium, built from SRTM, GMTED and ETOPO1), land, lakes, rivers, roads, urban areas, towns, countries and seas from [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/) 1:10m, and NASA's Blue Marble (colour) and Black Marble (night lights). `python3 tools/bake-earth.py <folder>` rebuilds `assets/` from them in about a minute; the script lists where to get each file. The baked map is about 4.5 MB (the game caches it for offline play).

The game started as Orbit Forge, a 2D rocket game, and was then called Rocket Science Simulator. It was renamed Overbuilt once it had grown well beyond rockets: the old name survives only in internal identifiers such as the `rss.` save keys and the `RSS1.`/`RSS2.`/`RSS3.` design codes, which stay so that saves and shared codes keep working. Before that, this repository held Cam-Strike, a 5v5 FPS. Its full history is still in git: the last version is commit `30e7bc8`.
